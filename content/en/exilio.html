The Cuban exile is not a Miami anecdote. It is the other half of this people: those who left with a suitcase, a child on a plane, or a raft, and did not ask the Party’s permission to stay Cuban. They built a life in the United States. They sent food to those who stayed. And they have not stopped calling the dictatorship a dictatorship.

The waves — 67 years of leaving

  1. 1959–62

    The founding exile

    After January 1, 1959, professionals, merchants, journalists and opponents leave. Pew counts about 250,000 Cubans arriving in the 1960s. They thought they would be back in months. They did not come back: the regime stayed.

  2. 1960–62

    Operation Pedro Pan

    14,048 children left alone, with visas and the Catholic Church. It is the largest exodus of unaccompanied minors in the hemisphere. Parents who preferred separation to a communist school. Many were not reunited for years.

  3. 1965–73

    Freedom Flights

    The Varadero–Miami airlift. More than 260,000 Cubans leave legally. Little Havana takes shape in Miami: Calle Ocho, Spanish on the street, politics as a family matter.

  4. 1980

    Mariel

    Castro opens the port. About 125,000 Cubans cross the strait in six months: workers, artists, opponents, and people the regime wanted to stigmatize. Miami changes scale.

  5. 1994

    The rafters

    The rafter crisis: 30,000 to 50,000 Cubans take to the sea. Many never arrive. The strait becomes a grave. Whoever touches land asks for asylum. Whoever does not, does not count.

  6. 2022–24

    The largest wave

    Pew: 2.9 million people of Cuban origin in 2024, 23% more than in 2019. 57% were born outside the U.S. They cross Nicaragua, Mexico, the Darién. It is not tourism: it is flight from hunger, blackouts and prison.

What they do — for Cuba and in the United States

For Cuba

Remittances, medicine, packages

The Havana Consulting Group estimated a peak of $3.717 billion in remittances in 2019 — and about $2.040 billion in 2022. It is not a Miami luxury: it is the rice, the antibiotic and the capital of a small business on the island. The state does not give it. A cousin sends it.

For Cuba

Naming the prisoners

From exile they press for Osorbo, Félix and Saylí Navarro, Ferrer, Henry Constantín. Cuban-American members of Congress, independent press (ADN Cuba and Martí) and marches on Calle Ocho. The regime calls them “worms.” They answer with names and years.

In the United States

A city built again

Miami went from a winter resort to a bilingual city. The Cuban Refugee Program, based at the Freedom Tower, served more than 443,000 people. 33% of Cubans 25 or older have at least a bachelor’s degree (Pew 2024). Doctors, teachers, builders, café owners.

In the United States

A political voice, without Havana’s permission

In 1989 Ileana Ros-Lehtinen becomes the first Cuban American in Congress. Jorge Mas Canosa founds the Cuban American National Foundation. Radio and TV Martí. Helms-Burton (1996) writes the embargo into law. It is not an abstract lobby: it is a people who do not accept the Party as the owner of Cuba.

Character

Against the dictatorship, without asking permission

The regime bet that time would soften them. It did not. Ninety miles away they still call prisoners prisoners, GAESA thieves, and the newscast a lie. Banishment, the “mercenary” label and the silence asked in exchange for a visa have not fully worked: every new wave arrives with the same rage.

  • They do not recognize the dictatorship as the nation

    For the exile, Cuba is the people — not the PCC. That is why Calle Ocho brings out flags when the island goes dark, and why banishment does not close a mouth: Otero and Ferrer speak from Miami.

  • Banishment is the price the regime charges

    Serving the sentence is not enough. In July 2026 they held Luis Manuel Otero Alcántara and expelled him. They offered Navarro freedom if he left: he said no. That is the measure of the character.

  • They did not leave the culture to the state

    Theater, rumba and journalism in Spanish, 90 miles away. The culture continued outside: it is not nostalgia, it is memory Granma cannot erase.

The exile, in public

Los Pichy Boys

Alejandro González and Maikel Rodríguez live in Miami and make Pichy Films under the name Los Pichy Boys. They are comedians. They are also one of the few popular voices of this exile that has kept a public record, with dates, against the Cuban dictatorship.

This page does not treat them as patrons or as a brand. It records what they have said in their own name: that July 11 was a demand for freedom, that political prisoners and the lack of food and medicine should not be left off camera, and that the reason so many Cubans live far from home is the Party, not the country they miss.

Their place in the community is not an office. It is a face a great many Cubans already know. For years Pichy Films spoke about the house, the split family and the island without asking the newscast for permission. When the subject stopped being the joke, they put that same audience on the prisoners, the hunger and the Party. That is the support on the record: a platform used, in public, on the side of Cubans who cannot speak.
