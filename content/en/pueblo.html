Sentenced on October 1

Anna and her mother

Anna y Caridad

Anna Sofía Benítez Silvente and Caridad Silvente

Anna is 21. She lives with her mother in Alamar, East Havana. On March 10, 2026 a Ministry of the Interior officer, Yoel Leodán Rabaza Ramos, came to their apartment to leave a State Security summons in the mother’s name. They filmed the visit and posted it. Since March 25 they have been under house arrest and cannot leave the country. The provincial prosecutor shelved the case on April 13 and reopened it on July 6, after the officer filed a complaint. The charge is “acts against personal and family privacy,” article 393 of the Penal Code. The September 17 hearing was postponed. On October 1, at the municipal court in Cojímar, both were found guilty. Anna told the press each of them got two years of limitation of liberty, and she called it an injustice. The officer’s lawyer had asked for prison for her. The court did not impose it. Their lawyer, Roberto Ortega, said limitation is not prison: they stay in ordinary life, under rules, including a ban on leaving the country. The written ruling comes in about 15 days. Then there are 10 business days to appeal.

Arrested the same day, on the way

The herald

Pregonero de Cristo

Pastor Rolando Fidel Pérez Lora

He is not a group. He is an evangelical pastor. The Cuban Observatory of Human Rights says he belongs to the Alliance of Christians of Cuba. On March 15, 2026 police arrested him in a park in Peñas Altas, Matanzas, after he uploaded a Bible teaching to YouTube. His wife, Gelayne Rodríguez Ávila, filmed officers pushing him into a patrol car. The Cuban Institute for Freedom of Expression and the Press and Christian Solidarity Worldwide documented it. On July 2 he and Anna Bensi were held for about twelve hours at the Alamar station. On the morning of October 1 he was going to Cojímar to stand with Anna and her mother. State Security and police threw him to the ground, handcuffed him and put him in a car. On the video he asks: “Am I being detained? You have to tell me why.” An officer answers: “It will be written up at the station.” CubaNet reported that his wife, Baptist leader David Espinosa and Espinosa’s wife were also detained. At the court, Anna denounced the arrest and the patrol cars around it.

CubaNet, 1 oct. 2026 · OCDH

The young men of the little room

El4tico

Ernesto Ricardo Medina and Kamil Zayas Pérez

Two young men from Holguín. El4tico is the little room in Piedra Blanca: a blackboard that often reads “Only Christ saves,” a broken fan, and short videos about daily life. Before the arrest the project had about 80,000 Instagram followers. On February 6, 2026 State Security took them from their homes and seized a laptop, camera, phone, tripod and router. Medina asked for the search warrant. They did not show it, and the search had no witnesses, against Law 143 of Criminal Procedure. They passed through the police unit known as “Todo el mundo canta.” By the end of March they were in Holguín Provincial Prison. The prosecutor charges propaganda against the constitutional order and incitement to commit a crime. Those charges can reach nine years. A habeas corpus was admitted and later rejected. In April, Medina’s mother said State Security demanded a video of repentance. He refused. Cubalex called that incompatible with freedom of conscience. As of September 6, 2026 they were still held, with no published prosecution request and no trial date. Kamil turned a year older on August 8 inside. Ernesto, on August 18. On October 2 ADN Cuba reported that the Holguín prosecutor, in conclusions dated September 15, asked for eight years for Medina and ten for Zayas. The court opened the trial. On October 7 Cubalex said those conclusions use the critical videos to build the charge. They are still in Holguín Provincial Prison.

Political prisoners, not statistics

The public list is read when the page opens. The August 2026 close was 1,339 — a record for the monthly report. 155 women. A 13-year-old boy. Name, years, and the Penal Code that manufactures them.

In Cuba there are no “common” prisoners who went out to ask for light. There is a Penal Code built for that: public disorder, contempt, assault, sedition, propaganda against the constitutional order, pre-criminal dangerousness. The trial is not a trial. The prosecutor dictates. The defense does not see the file. Banishment is offered as a favor.

Prisoners Defenders closed August 2026 with 1,339 verified political prisoners — the highest figure in decades. That month: 19 added, 7 released. In twelve months, 281 new, more than 23 a month. Since July 2021 they total 2,167 people. Of the 1,339, 807 are prisoners of conscience, 495 convicted of conscience and 37 other cases. Javier Larrondo: “Asking for water or electricity can land you in prison.” Public list, read when this page opens: 1,355 names, noviembre 2026. 19 new this month. The August 2026 report still closed at 1,339.

Inside the prison

Cubalex counted nine simultaneous hunger strikes and five deaths in custody in August. Kilo 8, in Camagüey, concentrated the cases. PD documents 463 prisoners with serious illness — hunger, beatings, neglect — and 54 with severe mental-health disorders, untreated. The European Parliament cited those figures on September 17 and asked to suspend the EU–Cuba agreement (374 votes in favor).

The hidden number

The 1,339 are the visible part. PD estimates at least 11,000 people under “pre-criminal conduct” files: prison for what the state says you might do. It is not a crime. It is a forecast. This page shows 17 named cases, not the full list.

Arrested as minors

Prisoners Defenders, list closed August 31, 2026: 38 people on it were arrested as minors — 36 boys and 2 girls. Fourteen are still behind bars. Sixteen of the July 11 minors were convicted of sedition; the average sentence is 5 years, harsher than what adult political prisoners received before 2021. Most of those 16 are now under house arrest or forced labor without internment. PD says the regime confirmed these figures in a report to the United Nations. In 2022 the Prosecutor General’s Office said it had prosecuted 55 people aged 16 to 18 for July 11. The “comprehensive training schools” that hold them answer to the Interior Ministry, not to Education. They are prisons with a school’s name. No photos of minors here.

The last great uprising of the people

July 11, 2021. It started in a town. Within hours it was the whole country. They shouted freedom. Díaz-Canel ordered them fought. They killed Diubis. Five years later, more political prisoners than ever.

It was the largest protest against the government since 1959. Larger than the 1994 Maleconazo, which fit on the seawall. This time the country came out: hunger, blackouts, a pandemic the state could not hide, and 62 years of one party. Miami did not call it. Hunger did. They shouted freedom. The answer was clubs, prison and a dead man with a name.

Five years later it is still the last great uprising this people has been able to make together. Not because the rage ended: because the dictatorship understood the message and answered with files, exile and fear. That is why this page tells July 11 with dates and sources. So it is not reduced to a tweet.

2021 · 11J

The island, the same day

One Facebook Live and, within hours, 13 provinces. They were not asking for a boat: they were asking for the country. The president gave a combat order. The internet went dark. Diubis fell the next day. That is the difference, and why it still hurts.

  1. Morning · 11:30

    It started in San Antonio de los Baños

    Sunday, July 11, 2021, a town 40 kilometers west of Havana. Three weeks of COVID lockdown, blackouts, lines, missing medicine. Hundreds came out on the main street: “freedom,” “Patria y Vida,” “Díaz-Canel, yéncate.” Yoan de la Cruz, 21, went live on Facebook. That broke a silence the state had held for 62 years.

  2. The hours

    From one town to the whole country

    The images jumped to Palma Soriano, Güira de Melena, Alquízar, the Malecón. Human Rights Watch documented abuses in 13 of 15 provinces and the Isle of Youth. No party called it. Neighbors, mothers, young, old. The Cuban flag — theirs, not the PCC's. Hands up. Unarmed. HRW: the protests were overwhelmingly peaceful.

  3. The order

    The president ordered his forces to fight the people

    Díaz-Canel went on national TV: “The combat order is given.” He was not talking about a foreign army. He was talking about his neighbors. He called “revolutionaries” to confront people asking for food and freedom. Black berets, police, rapid-response brigades. ETECSA cut the internet so the world would not see. They arrested those going to protest and those filming.

  4. July 12

    Diubis Laurencio Tejeda, 36

    A singer. La Güinera, Arroyo Naranjo, Havana. A policeman shot him in the back, according to the Cuban Observatory of Human Rights. HRW names him as the protester killed by police. The state spoke of riots. His family asked for justice. He has a name. He is not a number.

  5. The years after

    Prison, banishment or silence

    Cubalex: more than 1,300 detained those days, with undercounting; a year later HRW cited more than 1,400, more than 700 still in prison. Courts confirmed more than 380 convictions, some of children; up to 25 years for “sedition” — throwing a stone. Military trials. Incommunicado, beatings, naked squats. Those who could not bear it left. Those who stayed learned the price.

  6. 2026

    Five years later, still the last great uprising

    There has been no other like it. The 1994 Maleconazo was Havana. July 11 was the island. Since then the regime bet on fear: more prisoners than ever (1,339 in August 2026), banishment in exchange for a cell, blackouts protested one street at a time and paid with a file. July 11 did not repeat in a single morning. It repeats, in pieces, every time a neighborhood comes out for power and is beaten back.

«La orden de combate está dada.»

Miguel Díaz-Canel · July 11, 2021 · national broadcast

That sentence is the regime's portrait: a president who declares war on his own people. That night they cut the internet. The next day, in La Güinera, they killed Diubis. Those who lived learned that asking for food could cost 25 years.

Who the dictatorship killed

8,267 documented deaths and disappearances since 1959. This page names the cases that have a date and a source. The full list is at Cuba Archive, not invented here.

No page can print every person the dictatorship killed. Cuba Archive, in its report of March 30, 2026, had documented 8,267 deaths and disappearances for political reasons from January 1, 1959 through March 15, 2026. It says the real number is higher. What follows are cases with a date and a public source. The rest of the names are in that database, not invented here.

The 115 are from Cubalex, February 1, 2024 to June 30, 2026: people who died in state custody. About 80 are tied to denied or negligent medical care, 21 to severe malnutrition or starvation, and 9 to direct lethal force. Cubalex does not call all 115 political. Santiago de Cuba had 29, Havana 21, Mayabeque 16.

  • 1959

    Pelotones de fusilamiento, 1959

    Cuba Archive documented 805 firing-squad executions in 1959 alone, plus 62 extrajudicial killings and 4 disappearances. The regime filmed them and ran them in movie theaters. Documented firing-squad executions since January 1, 1959: 3,084. They do not fit on this page: each name is in their database.

  • July 13, 1994

    Remolcador 13 de Marzo

    41 people drowned, children among them, when state boats rammed the tug leaving Havana and turned water cannons on it. The Inter-American Commission recorded it as case 11.436, report 47/96.

  • February 24, 1996

    Armando Alejandre, Carlos Costa, Mario de la Peña y Pablo Morales

    Brothers to the Rescue. Two unarmed civilian planes were shot down by Cuban MiGs over the Florida Straits. All four were killed. ICAO and the United Nations condemned the attack.

  • February 23, 2010

    Orlando Zapata Tamayo

    A mason and a prisoner of conscience. He died in a Camagüey hospital after 85 days on hunger strike in Kilo 8 prison. Amnesty International had named him a prisoner of conscience. He was 42.

  • January 19, 2012

    Wilman Villar Mendoza

    Age 31. Arrested at a protest in Contramaestre and sentenced to 4 years. He died in Santiago de Cuba during a hunger strike, without care. Amnesty documented it.

  • July 22, 2012

    Oswaldo Payá y Harold Cepero

    Payá, leader of the Varela Project, and Harold Cepero Escalante died near Bayamo when a car forced them off the road. In 2023 the Inter-American Commission held the Cuban state responsible. The regime called it an accident.

  • August 7, 2020

    Yosvany Aróstegui Almenteros

    He died in the prison wing of Amalia Simoni hospital, in Camagüey, after about 40 days on hunger strike. Cuba Archive records it as a death in custody.

  • July 12, 2021

    Diubis Laurencio Tejeda

    Age 36. He was shot in the back in La Güinera, Havana, the day after July 11, in an unarmed protest. He is the person killed in the street during those days. OCDH and Human Rights Watch name him.

  • November 30, 2024

    Manuel de Jesús Guillén Esplugas

    Age 30. An 11J prisoner, linked to UNPACU. He died in Combinado del Este prison after a beating by guards. The official account said suicide. Cuba Archive dates it November 30.

  • February 11, 2025

    Yoleisy Oviedo Rodríguez

    Age 44. A political prisoner. She died at the El Guatao forced-labor camp amid reports she was denied medical care. At first the family could not see the body. Cuba Archive and Cubalex.

  • February 15, 2026

    Luis Miguel Oña Jiménez

    Age 27. An 11J prisoner, sentenced to 12 years for sedition. He suffered a stroke in a prison for HIV patients. He was given a terminal diagnosis, sent home on parole, and died shortly after. Cuba Archive.

  • May 12–13, 2026

    Ernesto Brieva Sempé

    Age 52, from Arroyo Naranjo. An 11J protester at El Toyo. Sentenced to corrective labor without internment and still locked in Combinado del Este for continuing to criticize. Cubalex found him severely malnourished. The official cause has not been verified. State Security watched the funeral.

Cubalex also counts, among 11J protesters who died under state responsibility, Luis Barrios Díaz, Geraldo Díaz Alonso, and Yosandri Mulet. Of Mulet, Cubalex says he took his own life while on a prison pass. That is not written here as a shooting. Six 11J protesters, by Cubalex’s May 2026 count, have died in that chain; Diubis was killed in the street the day after the protest.
