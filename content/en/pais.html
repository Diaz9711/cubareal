It is not one blackout. It is a country falling apart at the same time: power, water, food, the hospital, the school, the street drug el químico on the corner, and a generation whose future was stolen. The elite keeps it that way because they lack nothing. Below, every figure has a photo and a source.

Power

8th

nationwide blackout of 2026 · Sept 18

September 18, dark again

On Friday, September 18, MINEM confirmed the total collapse of the national grid: the eighth nationwide blackout of the year. That day at 06:00 UNE had 1,113 MW available against 2,686 MW of demand. For the peak it forecast 1,233 MW against 3,210 MW: 2,007 MW short. On Thursday the shortfall hit 2,051 MW. On September 22 Havana still had 105 circuits and 336 MW affected (Empresa Eléctrica). Matanzas has gone as long as 87 hours without power (OCHA).

Scarcity

8 in 10

skipped a meal

The ration shop is empty

94% live in extreme poverty, according to OCDH’s ninth study (1,318 interviews, June–July 2026). The ration book does not last the month. Oil, milk, chicken and soap are luxuries. What appears, appears in dollars. Anyone paid in pesos is left out.

Water

3.1 M

facing water cuts · Sept 2026

No power, no water

More than 3.1 million Cubans face total or partial cuts. The National Institute of Hydraulic Resources admits more than 40% of pumped water is lost. In Havana, 40 to 70% never reaches the tap. 87% of supply depends on the grid: when the lights go, the water goes.

Hospitals

30%

of the basic drug list, available

Surgery by phone light

Díaz-Canel and MINSAP admit only 30% of the basic drug list is available: seven in ten essential medicines are missing. 100,000 surgeries waiting, about 12,000 of them children. Infant mortality went from 4.0 in 2018 to 9.9 in 2025. Patients buy supplies on the black market. Chikungunya, dengue and oropouche run through the country.

Schools

21,000

teachers missing

Classrooms without a teacher, without power, without a snack

School year 2026–2027: 1.4 million students and only 73% of teaching posts filled, minister Naima Trujillo acknowledged. Havana brought in 3,000 teachers from the east. Blackouts, buildings falling down, shortened days. “Free” education no longer teaches.

Drugs

+200%

rise in use since the pandemic

The street drug el químico is in the neighborhood

Toxicologist Alejandro Mestre Barroso, opening the Las Tunas Drug Observatory, put the rise at more than 200% compared with before the pandemic. The hardest hit are ages 14 to 25. Cases start at age 8. In 2024 more than 90% of the poisonings treated by the National Toxicology Center were synthetic drugs.

Youth

13–24%

of the population left since 2020

From bad to worse

No school that works, no job that pays, the street drug el químico around the corner, and a police force that can be bought. Foreign Affairs puts the exodus since 2020 at 13 to 24% of the population, mostly young. ONEI put the island at 9.43 million at the end of 2025.

What leaves the island

Sold abroad. Not on the ration book.

Tobacco, coffee and rum still carry Cuba’s name in foreign shops. The premium brand is a state business. What reaches the bodega is a mix, a late delivery, or an empty shelf. Not every leaf is exported. The luxury product is.

What happened: sugar was the spine of the economy for two centuries. In the late 1980s, with the Soviet buyer, the harvest was about 8 million tonnes. That market ended in 1991. In 2002 the government closed 71 of 156 sugar mills and laid off about 100,000 workers (El País, June 9, 2002). By 2025 The Guardian put the harvest near 165,000 tonnes. No sugar, no molasses, no rum. Coffee’s modern peak was about 60,326 tonnes in 1961; the low point in the series was 4,973 tonnes in 2012 (International Journal of Cuban Studies, 2024). In 2010 Raúl Castro said the pea mix was unavoidable. The cigar is the exception: the Habano is still sold as a luxury abroad. What broke is the pack at home, and the volume.

827 M

Tobacco

Habanos S.A., half owned by the Cuban state, reported 827 million dollars in sales in 2024, 16% more than in 2023. The company said so on February 24, 2025, at the Habano Festival. China, Spain, Switzerland, the United Kingdom and Germany led the markets (Habanos; Reuters). On the island the pack is missing. IPS, using ONEI figures: the 2024 plan was 830 million packs, mostly for Cuba, and production reached 74%. On the informal market a pack cost 450 to 1,000 pesos (IPS, March 2025). elTOQUE, February 2025, found the same gap, 400 to 1,000 CUP. ONEI also said that in 2023, 55.9% of 395 million tobacco units went to national consumption. The export is the luxury cigar. The cigarette on the shelf does not keep up.

−51%

Coffee

March 13, 2025: coffee does not reach the bodegas. People notice it has disappeared from the ration, while the informal price goes up. ONEI: production fell 51% in five years. When the ration packet did exist, it was not coffee: elTOQUE, September 21, 2023, described it as 50% coffee and 50% peas, 115 grams, only for people over seven. In December 2010 Raúl Castro said the country could not spend almost 50 million dollars importing coffee and had to mix it with peas (Diario de Cuba, quoting that speech). The monthly cup on the ration book is no longer something a family can count on.

165.000 t

Rum

The Guardian, May 30, 2025, citing a Reuters analysis: Azcuba was heading for about 165,000 tonnes of sugar in 2025. In the late 1980s the harvest was about 8 million. Cuba imports sugar for the table. Without molasses there is no rum. An industry executive, unnamed, told The Guardian that in the fourth quarter of 2025 “there will be no alcohol.” The brands — Havana Club with Pernod Ricard, and others with Diageo and LVMH — are joint businesses of the state and foreign companies. The daiquiri on the poster is not the one in the bodega.

The accounts

Where the money is

A Miami Herald leak, on August 6, 2025, put about $18 billion in GAESA’s current assets as of 2024. The government denies it. Separately, Díaz-Canel and MINSAP have said only 30% of the basic drug list is available, and OCDH’s ninth study found 94% in extreme poverty.
