The president now

Miguel Díaz-Canel Bermúdez

1960–

He has been president of the Republic since October 10, 2019. From April 19, 2018 he already chaired the Council of State. On April 19, 2021 Raúl handed him the post of first secretary of the Party. He did not come out of an election with more than one party. The same power that has run the country since 1959 appointed him.

On July 11, 2021, with people in the street, he spoke on national television. The Foreign Ministry site published the line: “the combat order is given: revolutionaries to the street.” He also said they would have to pass over his dead body if they wanted to bring down the Revolution. That order sent his people to confront those who were protesting. The arrests and the prisoners of that day are in The people section of this page, with names and sources.

He did not inherit a country and find it this way. He accepted the office, the Party and the national broadcast. On July 11 he used all three to send one Cuban against another. The United States did not charge him with a crime. On June 4, 2026 it sanctioned him: it froze any property he holds under U.S. jurisdiction and barred its companies and citizens from dealing with him. The same list reached his wife, Lis Cuesta. The Associated Press reported it that day.

2015. Presidency of El Salvador. Public domain, CC0.

Prime minister

Manuel Marrero Cruz

2019–

He chaired Gaviota, the military hotel chain. Later, as prime minister, the state kept putting the money there. On December 20, 2024 El País reported that almost 40% of public investment that year went to tourism, with fewer visitors and half-empty hotels. In 2021, with him at the head of the government, hotel occupancy was 11.4% and still 37.6% of state investment went to hotels. The Miami Herald reported those official figures. Health and education, together, received a fraction.

GAESA’s leaked statements, published by the Miami Herald on August 6, 2025, give Gaviota a 42% profit margin in the first quarter of 2024. Almost four times the world hotel average. That margin belongs to the company he ran and to the policy he kept defending, while the neighborhood was in the dark. On July 14, 2025, before the Assembly, he repeated that prosperous tourism would bring income and that looking after the hotels was not neglecting the people.

On July 11, 2021 he was the head of government. The president gave the combat order, on national television. The arrests of that day were made with Marrero in office. There is no United States criminal charge against him. The harm on the record is the money: hotels full of cement, and the people without power and without food.

September 28, 2022, Tokyo. Cabinet Public Affairs Office of Japan. CC BY 4.0.

The foreign minister

Bruno Eduardo Rodríguez Parrilla

2009–

On March 3, 2010, at the UN Human Rights Council, he said: “I do not recognize the existence of political prisoners.” He added that they were people tried for illegal acts, with every guarantee. Orlando Zapata had died on February 23, after more than eighty days on hunger strike. Bruno said the government had respected the law and humanitarian principles. EFE published it that day.

On July 11, 2021 the combat order was published on the site of the ministry he runs. On January 24, 2022 he wrote that the trials of that day’s protesters “are conducted in full accordance with the law and within internationally accepted standards.” Those trials are the ones in The people section. He called them legal.

On May 26, 2026, at the Security Council, he called the indictment of Raúl a pretext and the energy measures genocide. On November 12, 2026, from China, he rejected the G7’s call to free the detainees and to hold an election with more than one party. He did not answer for the prisoners. He again called the embargo genocide. The United States did not charge him in case 03-20685. What is written is the denial.

March 26, 2013, United Nations. Argentine Foreign Ministry. CC BY-SA 2.0.

The one in command

Fidel Castro

1926–2016

On June 22, 1989 the Party paper, Granma, printed what the government could no longer deny. High officers, among them General Arnaldo Ochoa and Colonel Antonio de la Guardia, had worked with the Medellín cartel. The Washington Post that day reported the government’s own figure: at least six tons of cocaine toward the United States since 1986, and $3.4 million taken for letting it through. De la Guardia’s group ran 19 operations between January 1987 and April 1989. Four failed. The rest went through. A shipment on April 10, 1987, 240 kilos, landed at the Varadero military base with a Cuban air-force escort. Julia Preston reconstructed it in The New York Review of Books.

Fidel said that he and Raúl learned of it after the arrests of June 12. That is his account, not proof that the operation could run in Cuba without the machine he commanded: a secret Interior Ministry department, built to evade the embargo, and a military airport. On July 13, 1989 the Council of State, which he chaired, did not commute the sentences. The Washington Post wrote that Castro said that even if he had the power to stop the firing squads, he would not. At dawn they shot Ochoa, Captain Jorge Martínez, De la Guardia and Major Amado Padrón. Pope John Paul II and Amnesty International had asked for clemency. There was none.

On July 13, 1994, about seven miles off the port of Havana, four state boats rammed and sank the tug 13 de Marzo. Seventy-two people who wanted to leave were on board. Forty-one died, ten of them children. Thirty-one survived. The Inter-American Commission on Human Rights established that in report 47/96, of October 16, 1996. On February 24, 1996 two Cuban MiGs shot down two unarmed civilian planes of Brothers to the Rescue. Carlos Costa, Armando Alejandre Jr., Mario de la Peña and Pablo Morales were killed. On June 28, 1996 ICAO concluded that one plane fell 9 nautical miles past the limit and the other 10, outside Cuban airspace, and that Cuba broke the rules by firing without warning and outside its jurisdiction. Havana said they had already been warned. Fidel was head of state and of the armed forces. The case the United States unsealed in 2026 charges Raúl. It names Fidel in the facts. He is not among the defendants: he died in 2016.

April 15, 1959, Washington. Library of Congress, U.S. News & World Report collection.

Oriente, January 1959

Raúl Castro

1931–

On January 14, 1959 the United States consulate in Santiago cabled the State Department. Seventy people had been shot in that city on the 12th. The trials ran under the Revolutionary Code of Justice of the Second Front, Raúl Castro’s, and he was in command of Oriente. The dispatch says that hurried justice was blamed on him.

Cuba Archive put names on that same night. Seventy-one men fell at San Juan Hill. A tribunal had condemned four in four hours. Raúl ordered the rest shot: if those four were guilty, all of them were. Father Jorge Bez Chabebe walked with them. On January 15, UPI quoted him saying 106 had already been executed in Oriente, and that more would follow. TIME, on January 26, placed the squads under his command, beside the grave.

He stayed minister of the Armed Forces from 1959 to 2008. Inside that army GAESA was built, the conglomerate that later took the hotels, the stores and the remittances, and that ended up run by his son-in-law, Luis Alberto Rodríguez López-Calleja. On June 14, 1989, with the cocaine case already open, Raúl spoke to the country on radio and television. The Washington Post recorded what he said that day: in military life the fear is that the enemy finds out first and publishes it. The court of honor that stripped Ochoa was made of 47 generals. On July 9 Fidel demanded the death penalty. On the 13th they were shot. The defense minister was not a spectator. On April 23, 2026 a Miami grand jury charged him, and the Justice Department unsealed the case on May 20. The counts are conspiracy to kill U.S. nationals, two of destroying aircraft and four of murder, for the February 24, 1996 shootdown. It is an indictment. It is not a conviction. Case 03-20685, Southern District of Florida.

September 28, 2015. Kremlin.ru, CC BY 4.0.

The grandson

Raúl Guillermo Rodríguez Castro

The Crab

He was born around 1985. He is the son of Déborah Castro Espín, Raúl’s daughter, and of General Luis Alberto Rodríguez López-Calleja, who ran GAESA until his death on July 1, 2022. In 2016 Raúl put him in charge of the General Directorate of Personal Security, the grandfather’s guard. He holds the rank of colonel in the Interior Ministry. There is no public office that explains the power. The nickname El Cangrejo is the one the independent press uses. It is not an official title.

The harm on the record is not a drug conviction. It is privilege next to hunger. GAESA, the conglomerate his father built under Raúl’s army, is estimated at 30 to 40 percent of the economy and close to 90 percent of retail, while the ration shop and the pharmacy are empty. An investigation by La Prensa of Panama, Armando.Info, Transparencia Venezuela en el Exilio and CLIP documented at least 13 of his private trips to Panama in 2024 and at least ten in the first nine months of 2025, on a Dassault Falcon 900EX registered in San Marino. People on the island cannot afford a ticket.

He holds no post in Díaz-Canel’s government. The BBC said so on March 13, 2026. Even so he is the grandson who controls who gets through to Raúl. When he is asked what he does at GAESA, he gives no title: he says he “supports” the monopoly. elTOQUE reported that from the interview by journalist Ruiz-Goiriena. After his father’s death, the executive presidency of GAESA passed to Ania Guillermina Lastres, and the same Panama investigation places her traveling with him. The family business has no window. The people stand in line. The United States did not charge him with a crime. The May 2026 indictment is against his grandfather.

2015, a visit to Mexico. Presidency of Mexico, CC BY 2.0.

La Cabaña

Ernesto Che Guevara

1928–1967

From January 3 to November 26, 1959 he commanded La Cabaña fortress, with a trip abroad from June 4 to September 8. The revolutionary tribunals worked there: at night, without the guarantees of a trial. Cuba Archive has documented 79 executions under his direct orders. Fifty-five fell between January and May, while he was in the fortress. Café Fuerte reports that count from the archive. It is not the only figure. José Vilasuso, a lawyer on the purge commission, told Álvaro Vargas Llosa that about 400 people were executed between January and the end of June. Cables from the United States embassy spoke of more than 500 in that period. Cuba published no official list. That is why this page gives the documented number and says who gives the others.

In April 1967 he wrote it without a detour, in the Message to the Tricontinental: “Hatred as an element of the struggle; a relentless hatred of the enemy, impelling us above and beyond the natural limitations of man and transforming him into an effective, violent, selective and cold killing machine.” It was not an image. It was the method.

In February 1957, in the Sierra, he ordered the execution of Eutimio Guerra, a peasant of the Rebel Army itself, accused of informing Batista’s government. Cuba Archive records what biographer Jon Lee Anderson found in Che’s private diary: he wrote that he fired the shot himself, with a .32 pistol, into the right temple, because nobody else would, and that he kept the dead man’s watch and belongings. At La Cabaña the method did not change. José Vilasuso, who prepared the case files, told Vargas Llosa of executions from Monday to Friday, at dawn, with the appeal confirmed automatically. Che told them to act on the conviction that all of them were guilty. The United States never opened a case against him. He had been dead since 1967.

June 2, 1959, during his command at La Cabaña.

What they refused to debate

The Varela Project

Not one of the seven. The citizen law they answered with prison.

11,020

Signatures delivered on May 10, 2002

  1. 1

    Speech and association

  2. 2

    Amnesty

  3. 3

    One’s own business

  4. 4

    A new election law

  5. 5

    A referendum

It took the name of Father Félix Varela. Article 88(g) of the Constitution said that 10,000 registered voters could propose a law. Oswaldo Payá and the Christian Liberation Movement used that article. On the morning of May 10, 2002, Payá, Regis Iglesias and Antonio Díaz Sánchez left 11,020 signatures at the National Assembly. The official who received them was Colombia Lugo Sánchez. Payá asked that the project be published and debated. The Movement itself wrote that day that it was already a bill.

The bill asked for five things: freedom of expression and association, amnesty for political prisoners, the right to own a business, a new election law, and a referendum so the people would vote. In June 2002 the Assembly did not debate it. It amended the Constitution to declare socialism irrevocable. In 2004 the Christian Liberation Movement reported a second delivery, about 14,000 more signatures. There was no debate then either. The European Parliament gave Payá the 2002 Sakharov Prize.

On March 18, 2003 the state arrested 75 opponents and sentenced them to between 6 and 28 years. Among them were organizers of the Varela Project, including Regis Iglesias and Antonio Díaz Sánchez, the same men who had delivered the signatures. It was called the Black Spring. On July 22, 2012 Oswaldo Payá and Harold Cepero died in a crash near Bayamo. Havana said it was a traffic accident. On June 12, 2023 the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights published report 83/23, case 14.196. It concluded that the state is responsible for those deaths. It weighed testimony that an official car rammed theirs. Cuba did not answer those arguments before the Commission.

The wars the country paid for

Angola and Ethiopia

In November 1975 Fidel sent the army to Angola. It was called Operation Carlota. Raúl was minister of the Armed Forces. On December 5, 1989 that ministry said 2,289 Cubans had died on those missions since 1959: nearly all in Angola, the rest in Ethiopia and other countries. From late 1977 Cuba fought the Ogaden war on Mengistu’s side. Havana published the figure. The Los Angeles Times reported it on December 6, 1989.
